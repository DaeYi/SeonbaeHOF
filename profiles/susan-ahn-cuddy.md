# Susan Ahn Cuddy

**Born:** January 16, 1915 — Los Angeles, California
**Died:** June 24, 2015 (age 100)
**Field:** US Navy officer, intelligence codebreaker, civil servant

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The first Asian American woman in the US Navy — rejected for her race, applied again, ended up training Navy combat pilots and breaking codes for the NSA.

## Story

Susan Ahn Cuddy was born in 1915, the daughter of Ahn Chang-ho — Dosan — one of the most consequential figures of the Korean independence movement, who died in a Japanese prison when Susan was in her twenties. Her parents had been the first Korean married couple to immigrate to the United States, in 1902. She grew up in Los Angeles, played baseball at Belmont High School, and became LA City College's first Asian American baseball player.

When the US entered World War II, she applied to the WAVES — the Navy's women's reserve. She was rejected because of her race. She applied again. They accepted her. She trained Navy pilots in air combat tactics at the Link Trainer program, became the Navy's first woman gunnery officer (an Asian American woman teaching men to shoot down enemy planes in 1943), and then transferred to the Office of Naval Intelligence, where her Korean language skills became operationally useful.

In 1947 she married Francis X. Cuddy, an Irish American Navy chief. To avoid Virginia's anti-miscegenation laws, they wed on a Washington DC naval base. That same year she joined what would become the National Security Agency, working as a codebreaker on classified projects. She retired in 1959, moved back to LA, and ran her family's restaurant in the San Fernando Valley until 1990.

She lived to 100. In her later decades she became a kind of institutional memory for Asian American military service — interviewed, honored, and consulted into her nineties, inducted into halls of fame she had been ineligible to join for most of her career.

## Decision moments

- Applied to the WAVES, was rejected for being Asian American, and applied again
- Took the gunnery officer post — a combat-adjacent role no Asian American woman had held — instead of safer administrative work
- Carried her father's independence-movement legacy into US military and intelligence service, rather than into Korean nationalist politics
- Married across racial lines in 1947 — on a military base, against state law, and stayed married for decades
- After retiring from intelligence work, ran the family restaurant — refused to be only the trailblazer of her early career
- Spent her last decades telling the story rather than disappearing into private retirement

## Why this matters for the next generation

Being told no is information about the system, not about you. The "first" of anything almost always happens because someone applied again after being rejected, then made the role bigger than the one they were originally allowed into. Susan Ahn Cuddy is also a reminder that Korean American history in the United States is much older than the post-1965 immigration wave many of us assume defines us. There were Koreans here in 1902. They had children. Those children fought wars, ran restaurants, broke codes, and lived to a hundred.
