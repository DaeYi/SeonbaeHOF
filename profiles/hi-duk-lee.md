# Hi Duk Lee (이희덕)

**Born:** July 29, 1939 — Tae Jung, Korea
**Died:** March 7, 2019 — Silver Lake, Los Angeles
**Field:** Entrepreneur, community builder, neighborhood developer

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The chemistry graduate who became a German coal miner who became the founder of LA's Koreatown — and who lost almost all of it before the city ever put up the signs.

## Story

Hi Duk Lee was born to farming parents in Korea, graduated from Chungnam National University in 1961 with a degree in chemistry, and left under Park Chung-hee's dictatorship in 1965. He studied hotel management in Switzerland, worked as a miner in West Germany — where he met his wife, Kil Ja, a nurse — and arrived in Los Angeles in 1968 with the post-1965 immigration wave. He worked as a factory line worker and a welder. At the time, the entire Korean population of Los Angeles was around 5,000 people, with no commercial center.

In 1971, Lee and Kil Ja put about $7,000 in savings plus a bank loan into Olympic Market at Olympic and Normandie — the first Korean-owned grocery in the neighborhood. The store struggled for three years, then turned profitable. Inspired by how Chinese immigrants had built Chinatown through architecture, signage, and food, Lee bought out the surrounding blocks, opened the VIP Palace / Young Bin Kwan restaurant — the first building in LA with traditional Korean tile architecture, with tiles he personally sourced from Korea — and developed VIP Plaza, a Korean-tenanted shopping center. His lobbying pushed LA County to formally recognize Koreatown as a neighborhood in 1980, when the city erected the blue Koreatown signs along Olympic Boulevard.

The signs went up just as Lee's empire came apart. A planned 230-room Korean hotel collapsed when interest rates climbed from 9% to 22%. He went bankrupt in 1982. He sold his commercial properties and left the country for roughly a decade, traveling Africa and China learning the tea trade and writing a 520-page memoir. He returned around 2002, settled in Silver Lake, and in his seventies opened Echo Garden, an eco-friendly nursery in Highland Park, which he called his greatest achievement. He deliberately stayed out of Koreatown for the rest of his life. He died of colon cancer in 2019, at 79. *"I've lost much money, but I don't regret what I've done since it all happened in Koreatown. My failures became fertilizer to bloom flowers."*

## Decision moments

- Left Korea under Park Chung-hee's dictatorship for Europe in 1965, taking the coal-mining route to get out
- Put nearly all his savings into a Korean grocery in a neighborhood that had no Korean grocery customers yet
- Bought up the blocks around Olympic Market and imported tiles from Korea for VIP Palace — built a neighborhood, not a business
- Pushed LA County for formal recognition of Koreatown as a neighborhood — a non-business move from a businessman
- Went forward with the hotel project as interest rates climbed; lost the empire in 1982
- After bankruptcy, left the country rather than stay and rebuild within Koreatown
- Returned in his sixties and opened a nursery instead of trying to recover what he had lost

## Why this matters for the next generation

The person who builds the neighborhood is often not the person who profits from it. Hi Duk Lee gave LA's Koreans a downtown — and lost his stake in it before the city's recognition signs went up. He chose, for the last twenty years of his life, not to walk back into the place he had built. His story complicates the immigrant founder myth: the founding is real, and so is the bankruptcy, and so is the nursery in Highland Park. Building something that outlives you can mean letting it outlive your ownership of it.
