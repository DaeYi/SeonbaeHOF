# K. W. Lee (이경원)

**Born:** June 1, 1928 — Kaesong, Korea
**Died:** March 8, 2025 — Sacramento, California
**Field:** Investigative journalist

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The Korean immigrant reporter who spent five years writing about one wrongfully convicted death-row inmate — and accidentally started the first pan-Asian American political movement.

## Story

Kyung Won Lee was born in 1928 in Kaesong, in what is now North Korea, under Japanese occupation. He immigrated to the United States in 1950 — the year the Korean War broke out — and studied journalism at West Virginia University, then took a master's at the University of Illinois. In 1958 the Charleston Gazette hired him: the first Korean immigrant on the staff of a mainstream daily newspaper in the continental United States. He spent his early career covering civil rights in the South, vote-buying in West Virginia, and the lives of coal miners in Appalachia. It was an unusual beat for an Asian American reporter in the early 1960s — and an unusual training ground for what would come next.

In 1970 he joined the Sacramento Union as an investigative reporter. In 1978 he started looking into the case of Chol Soo Lee, a young Korean immigrant on California's death row for a Chinatown gang murder he insisted he hadn't committed. K.W. Lee ended up writing more than 120 articles over five years. Asian American social workers, students, and grandmothers — Korean, Chinese, Filipino — photocopied his pieces and passed them around. A defense committee formed. The conviction was overturned. Chol Soo Lee was acquitted at retrial in 1983. The movement that coalesced around the case is widely credited as the first sustained pan-Asian American political movement in US history. Hollywood eventually turned the story into the 1989 film *True Believer*.

Lee was not satisfied with that. In 1979 he founded Koreatown Weekly, the first national English-language Korean American newspaper. In 1990 — as tensions between Black and Korean Angelenos were building toward what would become Sa-I-Gu — he launched and edited the English edition of the Korea Times in Los Angeles, with an internship program built specifically to train Asian American and minority journalists. He was the founding president of the Korean American Journalists Association and the first recipient of AAJA's Lifetime Achievement Award. He survived liver and stomach cancer and a 1992 liver transplant. He died in Sacramento in March 2025, at 96.

## Decision moments

- Immigrated alone in 1950 — into a country at war with the country next to his — and chose journalism, a field where almost no one looked like him
- Took the Appalachia and Deep South civil rights beats in his first decade as a reporter, instead of any of the safer assignments available
- Decided, at the Sacramento Union in 1978, that Chol Soo Lee's case was worth five years and 120 articles
- Founded English-language Korean American media (Koreatown Weekly, Korea Times English Edition) when the community had almost no mainstream voice of its own
- Built an internship pipeline at Korea Times specifically to put more Asian American reporters into mainstream newsrooms
- Kept teaching and mentoring into his nineties — the "godfather" role wasn't an honorific, it was a daily practice

## Why this matters for the next generation

One reporter, one story, can move history — but only if the reporter stays on it long enough that the story stops being a story and becomes a movement. K.W. Lee's career is a master class in what investigative journalism actually costs: years, not weeks; one case, not many; obsessive specificity, not breadth. He is also a reminder that the pan-Asian American political identity that today's Korean Americans take for granted was not inherited. It was built — and a Korean immigrant reporter writing about a Korean inmate in a California prison turned out to be one of the people who built it.
