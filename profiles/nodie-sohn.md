# Nodie Kim Sohn (노디 김 / 김혜숙)

**Born:** October 29, 1898 — Koksan, Hwanghae Province, Korea
**Died:** 1972
**Field:** Educator, independence-movement organizer, postwar Korean civil servant

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

Came to Hawaii at six, graduated Oberlin at nineteen, became the first Korean woman principal in the islands at twenty — and ran a school tied so closely to Syngman Rhee that her story can't be told without his.

## Story

Born Kim Hyesuk in Hwanghae Province in 1898, Nodie Kim arrived in Hawaii in 1905 — part of the first wave of Korean immigration, a six-year-old in a sugar-plantation territory. She grew up in the Korean Christian community in Honolulu, was sent to Oberlin College in Ohio, and graduated in 1918 at age nineteen. She came straight back to Hawaii and took the principalship of the Korean Christian Institute, the school Syngman Rhee had broken off from the Methodist mission to found a few years earlier. She was the first Korean woman to serve as a principal in the territory. She held the job, on and off, until 1945.

The KCI was not a neutral institution. It was Rhee's school — the operational base of his Hawaii faction and the educational arm of the Dongji Hoi (Comrade Society), the political organization through which he ran his wing of the exile independence movement. Nodie Kim was its principal, the president of the Korean Ladies Relief Society, and a "manager and liaison officer" of the Dongji Hoi. She was, in other words, Rhee's institutional infrastructure in Hawaii for a quarter century. When Rhee returned to Korea and became its first president, that closeness paid out: in 1952 she went to Seoul, was appointed director of the Republic of Korea Office of Procurement, and became vice-president of the Korean Red Cross.

In 2021, alongside Chung Song Lee Ahn, she was posthumously honored by the South Korean government as a "woman patriot" at a ceremony on the steps of the UH Mānoa Center for Korean Studies, with President Moon Jae-in present. By that point her name had cleanly entered the independence-movement pantheon — the harder political questions about the Rhee era, which her career was structurally tied to, kept to the footnotes.

## Decision moments

- Sent from Hawaii to Ohio for college in the 1910s — and came back, when many Korean American graduates of that era stayed on the mainland
- Took the KCI principalship at nineteen, in a movement-defining institution led by Syngman Rhee
- Stayed inside Rhee's organizational orbit (Dongji Hoi, Korean Ladies Relief Society, KCI) for the entire interwar period
- Took an appointment in Seoul in 1952 under the Rhee government, rather than retire in Hawaii after the war
- Returned to Hawaii later in life, where she died in 1972 — outside the political fallout that consumed Rhee's reputation

## Why this matters for the next generation

Diaspora politics is always factional politics, and being effective inside a faction is not the same as being on the right side of history. Nodie Kim was extraordinarily capable — first woman to do almost everything she did, two decades running a school, a postwar government post in the country she'd left at six — and her career is also a study in what it costs to be that close to a single leader. The South Korean state has now honored her. Historians of the Rhee era will continue to complicate that honor. Both readings are real. For the next generation: there is no clean institutional life. The institutions you build inside are also the politics you inherit.

## Red flag check

The Dongji Hoi / Syngman Rhee connection is the central complication. Rhee's later presidency (1948–1960) is one of the most contested legacies in modern Korean history — authoritarian rule, the Jeju and Bodo League massacres, electoral fraud that triggered the April Revolution. Nodie Kim's exile-era institutional work predates and is distinct from those events, but her 1952 procurement appointment in Seoul came under Rhee's government. Worth foregrounding rather than burying when this profile is used in any public-facing context.
