# Dae-Sook Suh (서대숙)

**Born:** November 22, 1931 — northern Korea (later North Korea)
**Died:** September 13, 2022 — Honolulu, Hawaii
**Field:** Political science, Korean Studies

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The Columbia-trained political scientist who built the first Korean Studies center in the United States — and wrote the definitive English-language biography of the man whose regime he had fled.

## Story

Dae-Sook Suh was born in the part of Korea that would become North Korea. In 1946, as the communist consolidation began, he defected south. During the Korean War he worked as an interpreter — one of the wartime jobs that funneled a generation of young Koreans into American universities. In 1952 he enrolled at Texas Christian University. He went on to Columbia, completing his PhD in Public Law and Government in 1964 with a dissertation titled "Korean Communism and the Rise of Kim." He had decided, early, that the regime that had shaped his life was a worthy object of cold-eyed scholarly study, not just political grievance.

In 1972 he moved to the University of Hawaii at Manoa to launch the Center for Korean Studies — the first dedicated Korean Studies center at any US university. He directed it from 1972 to 1995. Over those 23 years he built a faculty, recruited graduate students, brought in visiting scholars from Seoul and Pyongyang-adjacent archives, and turned an interdisciplinary affiliation network into what is still the largest Korean Studies center in North America. His two best-known books, *The Korean Communist Movement, 1918–1948* and *Kim Il Sung: The North Korean Leader* (Columbia University Press), remained for decades the standard English-language works on their subjects.

His scholarly authority came from a particular kind of intellectual discipline: refusing to let his biography determine his analysis. He was a North Korean refugee writing the most careful available account of North Korea's founder. The Center for Korean Studies marked its 50th anniversary in September 2022; Suh was honored posthumously, having died that month.

## Decision moments

- Defected from the North as a teenager — a permanent rupture with his birthplace
- Chose to study the Korean communist movement academically rather than politically, despite his personal history with it
- Took the Hawaii job in 1972 to start a center from nothing, when "Korean Studies" was not yet a recognized US field
- Stayed 23 years as director, prioritizing institution-building over personal scholarly mobility
- Wrote a biography of Kim Il Sung that aimed for the standard of a Columbia University Press monograph rather than a polemic

## Why this matters for the next generation

Some of the most useful work a scholar can do is to write the book the field needs before the field exists. Suh's life shows what it looks like to convert personal history — exile, war, displacement — into a professional discipline that outlasts the displaced. The Center for Korean Studies trained generations of scholars who now hold chairs across the US and Asia. None of those careers would have looked the same without his decision, in 1972, to take a job in Honolulu and stay.

## Red flag check

His scholarship on Kim Il Sung was occasionally contested by South Korean nationalist historians who saw it as too willing to treat North Korean sources as serious historical material. This was, in fact, the methodological choice that made his work durable.
