# Young Oak Kim (김영옥)

**Born:** April 29, 1919 — Los Angeles, California
**Died:** December 29, 2005 — Los Angeles, California
**Field:** US Army officer, civic leader

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

A Korean American officer who refused to leave a Japanese American battalion in 1943 — and went on to become the first minority officer in US history to command a combat battalion.

## Story

Young Oak Kim grew up in Bunker Hill, the son of Korean immigrants who ran a grocery store at Temple and Figueroa. His father struggled with a gambling problem; the family struggled with money; LA in the 1930s offered a Korean kid almost no steady work no matter how well he did at Belmont High. When he tried to enlist in the Army, he was turned away because of his race. He entered only after Congress, in 1941, conscripted Asian Americans.

After Officer Candidate School at Fort Benning, Kim was assigned to the 100th Infantry Battalion — an all-Japanese American unit. His commander offered him a transfer out, worried about ethnic tension between a Korean officer and Japanese American troops at the height of a war in which Japan still occupied Korea. Kim refused. *"There are no Japanese nor Korean here,"* he said. *"We're all Americans and we're fighting for the same cause."* With the 100th and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team he fought through Italy and France, crawling into German lines in daylight at Cisterna with Private Irving Akahoshi to capture the prisoners whose intelligence helped open the road to Rome. He came home with the Distinguished Service Cross, two Silver Stars, three Purple Hearts, the French Croix de Guerre and Légion d'honneur — and a 19-medal record that his country had not been willing to let him earn five years earlier.

He left the Army, built a profitable laundry business, then walked away from it when the Korean War began. By 1951 he was an intelligence officer with the 31st Infantry in Korea. After he was nearly killed by friendly fire from his own artillery, his commanding general promoted him to major and gave him the 1st Battalion — making him the first minority officer in US history to command a combat battalion. His battalion adopted a Seoul orphanage of more than 500 children. He retired in 1972 with 80% disability and spent the next thirty years building Los Angeles nonprofits — Korean Youth and Cultural Center, Korean Health Education Information and Research Center, the Korean American Coalition, the Center for Pacific Asian Families. He died at 86. UC Riverside's Young Oak Kim Center for Korean American Studies, and an LA middle school, carry his name.

## Decision moments

- Got turned down by the Army for being Asian, waited until conscription forced them to take him, and went anyway
- Refused the transfer out of a Japanese American battalion at a moment when Korea was under Japanese occupation — chose American identity over ethnic grievance, in uniform, in 1943
- Volunteered for daylight reconnaissance behind German lines at Cisterna
- Walked away from a thriving laundry business to fight in Korea, citing his dual debts as Korean and as American
- Accepted command of the 1st Battalion of the 31st Infantry — and made the historical first happen on the ground, not on paper
- Spent the entire second half of his life building LA nonprofits, not chasing prestige appointments or military consultancies

## Why this matters for the next generation

The cost of being a "first" is often paid before the title shows up. Young Oak Kim was rejected for his race, fought in a segregated unit, was nearly killed by his own side's artillery, then was given a battalion to command — in that order. He could have lived off the medals. Instead he spent thirty years building the unsexy infrastructure of Korean Los Angeles: a youth center, a health center, a women's shelter, a coalition. He understood that being the first doesn't matter unless what you build outlasts your name on it.
