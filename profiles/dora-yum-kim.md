# Dora Yum Kim (염도라)

**Born:** 1921 — San Francisco, California
**Died:** 2003
**Field:** Social worker, community organizer, real estate broker

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The daughter of a Korean independence organizer who placed over 3,000 Korean immigrants into their first American jobs — and built the Bay Area's first Korean community center as the second job she did after her first one.

## Story

Dora Yum Kim was born in 1921 in San Francisco's Chinatown, one of roughly 100 Koreans in the city — a Korean minority inside a Chinese immigrant minority inside white America. Her father, Yum Man Suk, was a leader of the Korean Independence Movement in California in the early 20th century. She grew up working in her parents' restaurant, was barred from most employment outside Chinatown because she was an "Oriental female," and started her working life as a typist at the California Department of Employment.

She worked her way up to social worker inside the same department. After the 1965 Immigration Act opened the country to Korean immigrants in large numbers, her Korean language and her years inside the state employment bureaucracy turned out to be exactly the combination the new arrivals needed. Between 1965 and 1977, she placed more than 3,000 Korean immigrants in their first American jobs. Along the way she became, in the 1950s, the first Asian woman real estate broker in San Francisco, and a leader of the United Nationalities of San Francisco — a multi-ethnic civic coalition decades before "AAPI" was a common phrase.

In 1976 she co-founded the Korean Community Service Center, the first Korean community center in the United States. She took early retirement from the state in 1977 to run it. She taught English to seniors, prepared them for naturalization exams, and built a senior meals program; she retired as the senior center coordinator in 1986. Her 1978 oral history, preserved at the San Francisco Public Library, remains one of the foundational primary sources on second-generation Korean American identity. Her biography, *Doing What Had to Be Done*, was the first published biography of an American-born Korean woman.

## Decision moments

- Took the typist job at the state employment department, the only kind of state work open to her, and stayed in the system long enough to become a social worker inside it
- Became San Francisco's first Asian woman real estate broker in the 1950s, in a profession that was structurally closed to her
- Used her position inside the California Department of Employment as an immigrant placement pipeline after 1965 — turning a government job into a one-woman resettlement service
- Sat for the 1978 oral history when no one was systematically collecting second-generation Korean American stories
- Co-founded the Korean Community Service Center in 1976 and took early retirement in 1977 to run it — left the state pension track to build an institution
- Stayed in service work as a senior center coordinator and later medical interpreter, instead of moving into more visible community leadership

## Why this matters for the next generation

Some of the most important Korean American institutions were built by people who never made it into a textbook. Dora Yum Kim spent forty years using a state employment office, a real estate license, and an oral history microphone as tools to make Korean American life in the Bay Area possible — and then built the community center that institutionalized the work. The title of her biography is a thesis statement: she didn't frame her life as ambition or activism. She framed it as the things that needed to happen, that no one else was going to do. That is also a model of leadership.
