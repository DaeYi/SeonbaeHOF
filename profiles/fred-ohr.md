# Fred Ohr (오정수)

**Born:** July 15, 1919 — Oregon (raised on a subsistence farm in the Boise Basin, Idaho)
**Died:** September 6, 2015 — Chicago, Illinois (age 96)
**Field:** US Army Air Forces fighter pilot, WWII flying ace; dentist

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The only American flying ace of Korean descent in World War II — a kid from an 8-by-10-foot tent in Idaho who shot down enemy planes over the Mediterranean, then quietly drilled teeth in Chicago for fifty-three years.

## Story

Fred Ohr was born in Oregon in 1919 and grew up on a hardscrabble farm in the Boise Basin in Idaho, where his entire family lived in an eight-by-ten-foot tent and worked the land by hand because they couldn't afford equipment. His father had fled Korea after the 1910 Japanese annexation; the family was wanted for execution back home. When Fred was six, he watched a postal biplane fly over the farm and decided he wanted to be a fighter pilot. His mother told him: if the desire is great enough, it will happen.

Getting into the cockpit took the kind of stubbornness that line implies. An Army Air Corps recruiter wordlessly pointed him toward the door. A Navy recruiter mocked him. He joined the Army in 1938 anyway and stumbled into a pilot exam in 1940 that finally redirected him toward flight training. He deployed to North Africa in 1942 as ground crew. His commanding officer, threatened by him, parked him in a maintenance role and refused to train him. When he finally got into the air with the 52nd Fighter Group, his squadron didn't accept him until he survived his first combat mission. He survived it. He survived 240 more. He flew Spitfires and then P-51 Mustangs with the yellow-tail markings of the 52nd, ended the war with six aerial kills and 17 ground kills, and rotated home in November 1944 as his squadron's commanding officer with two Silver Stars, two Distinguished Flying Crosses, a Bronze Star, and 18 Air Medals.

He met Esther Kang in Chicago in January 1945, married her in 1946, and walked away from the Air Force to raise a family. He used the GI Bill to attend UC Berkeley and then Northwestern University Dental School, opened a Chicago practice in 1950, and ran it for fifty-three years until retiring in 2003. He lived another twelve years and died at 96 — Major Fred Ohr, USAAF, DDS.

## Decision moments

- Applied to the Army Air Corps after being silently turned away, then to the Navy after being mocked, then to the Army — kept walking back into recruiters' offices until one of them let him in
- Took the maintenance assignment in North Africa his commander used to sideline him, and stayed close enough to flying that when an opening came he was ready
- Flew 241 combat missions in a unit that initially refused to accept him, until his survival did the convincing
- Walked away from a likely peacetime Air Force career at the end of WWII to start a family and a civilian profession
- Chose dentistry — quiet, daily, unspectacular — after four years of being one of the most visible fighter pilots in the Mediterranean theater
- Practiced for fifty-three years in the same Chicago neighborhood, retiring at 86

## Why this matters for the next generation

The first part of the story is the dramatic one — the Korean kid from an Idaho tent becoming a flying ace despite recruiters who wouldn't even speak to him. The second part is the more useful one. Fred Ohr did the hardest thing a war hero can do, which is stop being a war hero. He came home, married, opened a dental practice, and lived a quiet civilian life for seventy years. The medals stayed in a box. The fight to get into the cockpit and the choice to leave it are the same kind of decision: deciding what your life is actually for, and then doing it whether or not anyone is watching.

Sources differ on his exact birthplace (Oregon vs. Idaho); the consensus is born in Oregon, raised in the Boise Basin. Aerial victory count (6) is well-documented in unit records; the 17 ground kills figure is standard in cited sources. None of the surfaced material flags controversy.
