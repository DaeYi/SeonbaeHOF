# K. Connie Kang (강경실)

**Born:** November 11, 1942 — Hamhung, Korea (now North Korea)
**Died:** August 16, 2019 — Los Angeles, California
**Field:** Journalist, author

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The first female Korean American journalist at US mainstream dailies — hired by the LA Times after the 1992 riots specifically because the paper had been covering Koreatown without anyone who could speak to it.

## Story

Connie Kang was born in 1942 in Hamhung, in what is now North Korea, into a Christian family. After the 1945 partition, her grandmother led the family across the 38th parallel to escape religious persecution. When the Korean War broke out in 1950, her mother and grandmother fled again — abandoning their possessions, traveling by train and fishing boat to Tokyo. She eventually immigrated to the United States, earned a journalism degree from the University of Missouri in 1963 and a master's from Northwestern, and in 1964 took her first reporting job at the Democrat & Chronicle in Rochester, New York. She was the first female Korean American journalist at a mainstream US daily. She was 21.

She moved west, joined the San Francisco Examiner, opened its Seoul bureau in time to cover the 1988 Olympics — "a dream come true," she called it — and later sat on the paper's editorial board, where she wrote an editorial apologizing for the Examiner's role in pushing Japanese internment in 1942. In 1982 she co-founded the Korean American Journalists Association. Then in 1992, after Los Angeles burned and the LA Times' coverage of Koreatown exposed how few of its reporters could even speak to the people involved, AAJA pushed the paper to hire her. She joined the Times that fall.

What happened next was, according to colleagues, "like night and day." Korean Americans across LA started calling her — because for the first time, somebody at the paper of record could take the call in their language. She covered the California Supreme Court (winning more than 30 awards), wrote thousands of stories about Korean and Asian American life, and in 1995 published *Home Was the Land of Morning Calm: A Saga of a Korean-American Family*, a memoir blending her family's three-country flight with the longer arc of Korea's struggle for independence and democracy. She won AAJA's Lifetime Achievement Award in 1997. Colleagues described her as methodical — "like an anthropologist" — and as a woman who wore wide-brimmed colorful hats and carried her Rolodex with her everywhere, a habit from her refugee past. She put it this way: *"I am more American than Korean in my mind, but I am more Korean than American in my soul."*

## Decision moments

- Started at a Rochester daily in 1964 — a 21-year-old Korean American woman in an industry that had room for almost no one like her
- Opened the SF Examiner's Seoul bureau and reported across the Pacific instead of taking the safer local-beat career
- Wrote, from the editorial board, an apology for her own newspaper's role in pushing Japanese internment — a confrontation with institutional history she did not have to undertake
- Co-founded the Korean American Journalists Association in 1982, building infrastructure for a community that didn't yet have it
- Accepted the LA Times job in fall 1992 — knowing the role was being created because the paper had failed Korean LA during the worst week of its history
- Wrote *Home Was the Land of Morning Calm* — turned her family's refugee story into public record rather than keeping it private

## Why this matters for the next generation

When a mainstream institution finally hires "one of you," the job that follows is harder than the hiring. Connie Kang walked into a newsroom that had been called out by name for failing her community, and her job — every day, for years — was to take the phone calls, write the stories, and slowly change what "Korean American" meant in the pages of America's second-largest newspaper. She is also the reminder that the most important Korean American journalism of the 1990s and 2000s was done by women — and that the memoir form, the long careful interview, the wide-brimmed hat and the Rolodex, are tools as serious as any byline.
