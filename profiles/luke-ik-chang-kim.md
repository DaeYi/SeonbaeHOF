# Luke Ik Chang Kim (김익창)

**Born:** April 22, 1930 — Sinuiju, Korea (now North Korea)
**Died:** July 12, 2015 — Seal Beach, California
**Field:** Cultural psychiatry, community activism

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The psychiatrist who treated Charles Manson and Sirhan Sirhan in a California prison — and who insisted, against the field's deepest habits, that you could not treat a patient without first understanding their culture.

## Story

Luke Kim grew up under three regimes in fast succession: Japanese occupation, Soviet-backed communism, and the Korean War. At fourteen he was conscripted by the Japanese into forced labor at a Pyongyang weapons factory. After liberation, his family fled south. During the war, his mother — a Presbyterian church leader — was kidnapped by North Korean authorities; he never saw her again. He finished medical school at Seoul National University in 1956 and came to the United States to do a PhD in clinical psychology at the University of Arizona, intending to return to Korea and build a psychology department there. His mentor died. He stayed.

For three decades he served as chief psychiatrist at the California Medical Facility in Vacaville, the prison hospital that housed many of the country's most notorious inmates. He treated Charles Manson, Sirhan Sirhan, Juan Corona, and Timothy Leary. From 1973 to 2005 he was clinical professor at UC Davis Medical School, where he became one of the first US psychiatrists to argue, in print and in training, that culture was not background context for mental illness but a constitutive part of it. He helped create the APA Task Force on Asian American Psychiatrists and, in 1997, was the first recipient of the APA's Kun-Po Soo Award for advancing the field's understanding of Asian issues.

He was not only a scholar. In the early 1980s, Kim and his wife Grace mortgaged their home to help fund the legal defense of Chol Soo Lee, a young Korean American wrongfully convicted of a San Francisco Chinatown murder — a campaign that became a generation-defining moment of Asian American civil rights organizing. In 2006 the Kims funded an endowed professorship at UC Davis to keep cultural psychiatry alive after he was gone.

## Decision moments

- Stayed in the US after his mentor's death instead of returning to build the Korean psychology department he had planned
- Took the chief psychiatrist post at a maximum-security prison hospital — career territory most US-trained psychiatrists avoided
- Pushed cultural psychiatry into the American Psychiatric Association at a time when "culture" was treated as a footnote, not a clinical variable
- Mortgaged the family home to fund Chol Soo Lee's defense — a personal financial bet on a stranger's freedom
- Endowed a chair at UC Davis so the field he helped create would outlast him

## Why this matters for the next generation

You can be a clinician and an activist in the same body — and the activism can be as technical as a diagnostic framework, not just a march. Kim's career argues that the most lasting form of cultural representation in a profession is to change how the profession works, not just who works in it. He treated some of America's most reviled people and fought for one of its most wronged. The thread was the same: people don't make sense outside their context.
