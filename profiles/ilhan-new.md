# Ilhan New (유일한 / Yu Il-han)

**Born:** January 15, 1895 — Pyongyang, Korean Empire
**Died:** March 11, 1971 — Seoul, South Korea
**Field:** Entrepreneur, pharmaceutical industry founder, OSS officer, philanthropist

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

Sent alone to America at nine, built a fortune on bean sprouts, spent it building Korea's first modern pharmaceutical company, and at age 50 parachute-trained with the OSS to be dropped behind Japanese lines into the country of his birth.

## Story

In 1904, a nine-year-old boy was put on a ship in Korea, alone, and sent to America. He landed in San Francisco and was placed with a family in Kearney, Nebraska, where he lived for seven years before moving to Hastings for high school — selling Eastern medicine to Chinese students, playing football well enough to earn a sports scholarship. He went on to study commerce at the University of Michigan and took a job at General Electric. Then, in 1922, he quit it. With his classmate Wallace Smith he founded La Choy Food Products, built around mung bean sprouts. By 1926 he had hundreds of thousands of dollars in the bank.

He took a trip to China that year and saw what Japanese colonial rule was doing to Korean health, and made the decision that organized the rest of his life. He liquidated his American business, returned to Seoul with $500,000, and in December 1926 founded Yuhan Corporation — the first Korean pharmaceutical company, the first to import modern medicines like prontosil into East Asia, eventually the first Korean firm listed on the Korea Stock Exchange. He built dormitories, childcare facilities, and swimming pools for his workers when Korean industrialists almost universally did not.

He kept moving between continents. He took an MBA at USC. In January 1945, at age 50 — well past the cutoff for combat fitness — Colonel Carl Eifler recruited him into Project NAPKO, the OSS plan to infiltrate Korean American and Korean ex-prisoner agents into Japanese-occupied Korea. He was inducted into the US Army in Washington, trained at Fort Meade and on Catalina Island in parachuting, sabotage, radio, and unarmed combat, and was preparing to lead agents into Korea when the atomic bombs ended the war on August 15, 1945. The first NAPKO drops had been scheduled for August 26. He returned to Korea in 1946, enrolled at Stanford Law in 1948 at age 53, and graduated in 1953. He gave his fortune away — to schools, to a technical high school he founded in 1953, ultimately to the Yuhan Foundation — and named a non-relative as his corporate successor, a choice still cited as the great counter-example in Korean chaebol succession history. His daughter Jae-Ra gave away her own inheritance to the same foundation when she died in 1991.

## Decision moments

- At nine years old, was put on a ship alone for America — and stayed, made a life there, built a fortune
- Walked away from General Electric in 1922 to sell bean sprouts to a country that didn't know it wanted them
- In 1926, sold the American business at its peak and moved back to Korea to start over in pharmaceuticals
- Built worker housing, childcare, and swimming pools for Yuhan employees in a colonial economy where this was unheard of
- At 50, volunteered for OSS combat training to be parachuted into Japanese-occupied Korea
- At 53, enrolled in Stanford Law School
- Wrote his fortune out of his family — to the foundation, to schools, to a non-relative successor — at a moment in Korean history when the chaebol model was being built on the opposite principle

## Why this matters for the next generation

Ilhan New is the Korean American figure who refused to choose. American by upbringing, Korean by mission, business by craft, military by improvisation, philanthropist by design — he ran the whole portfolio at once, and the through-line was always the same: what does this country, in this decade, actually need from someone in my position? The answers kept changing. In 1922 it was bean sprouts. In 1926 it was modern medicine. In 1945 it was a parachute. In 1953 it was a technical high school. In 1971 it was a foundation big enough to outlive him. He is the case study in treating your own life as a resource to be deployed against the problem of the moment — and in trusting that what you owe your family is best paid forward, not down.

## Red flag check

Yuhan Corporation's Korean-language hagiography of New is necessarily celebratory; the broader academic literature (Business History Conference paper, Smithsonian piece) is more measured and substantiates the major claims here. No serious controversy surfaced. New is borderline on the strict "primarily lived/contributed in the US" rule — he spent the last decades of his life in Korea — but he was sent to the US at age 9, naturalized, built and sold an American business, served in the US Army, and earned a Stanford JD; he meets the working interpretation.
