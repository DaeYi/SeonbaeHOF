# Kim Chong Lim (김종림)

**Born:** 1884 — Korea
**Died:** 1973 — Los Angeles, California
**Field:** Rice farmer, independence funder, founder of the first Korean aviation school

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The first Korean American millionaire — a Sacramento Valley rice baron who funded the birth of the Republic of Korea Air Force and lost the entire fortune in a single flood.

## Story

Kim Chong Lim arrived in San Francisco aboard the Alameda in 1906, age 22. His first American job was laying railroad track in Salt Lake City. Within a year he was already mailing donations to Korean nationalist newspapers — $10 to the Gongnip Association in 1907, $30 for printing equipment in 1908. By the mid-1910s he had moved to Willows, in the Sacramento Valley, and started growing rice. In 1915 he farmed 100 acres. Four years later, in 1919, he was farming over 3,300 acres. The Korean American community called him the Rice King. His annual income ran into the hundreds of thousands of dollars at a time when most Korean immigrants were doing seasonal stoop labor.

In early 1920, General Noh Baek-rin came through Willows on behalf of the new Korean Provisional Government in Shanghai. He told Kim, *"The victor of the next war will be the one who rules the sky."* Kim donated forty acres of his own land for an airfield, put up $20,000 in startup cash, pledged $30,000 a year in operating costs, hired American flight instructors, bought three aircraft, and paid every cadet's room, board, and tuition. The Willows Korean Aviation School opened February 20, 1920, with fifteen students. By June there were thirty. Kim served as its president. The Republic of Korea Air Force today traces its institutional lineage directly to this school.

In October 1920, a catastrophic flood hit the Sacramento Valley before harvest. Kim's rice fields — all 3,300 acres of them — were destroyed. The fortune was gone in a week. Without its underwriter, the aviation school limped through another season and was disestablished in April 1921. Kim never rebuilt the empire. He enlisted in the California State Guard during World War II, in his late fifties. His two sons served in the US Navy. He lived quietly in Los Angeles and died in 1973. South Korea awarded him the Order of Merit for National Foundation in 2005. In 2009, his remains were transferred to the National Cemetery in Daejeon.

## Decision moments

- Mailed $10 to a Korean nationalist newspaper in 1907 while working as a railroad laborer — committed to independence funding before he had any money to give
- Bet his agricultural future on rice in Willows in the mid-1910s, scaling from 100 to 3,300 acres in four years
- After one conversation with General Noh in 1920, committed roughly $50,000 plus 40 acres of land — most of a year's profits — to a flight school for a country he had not seen in 14 years
- When the October 1920 flood wiped him out, did not abandon his community obligations — kept giving what he could, just at a fraction of the old scale
- Enlisted in the California State Guard in his late fifties during WWII, served the country he had migrated to

## Why this matters for the next generation

Wealth that is held for its own sake is one thing. Wealth that is deployed at scale toward a cause that may never pay back is another. Kim Chong Lim made a decision in February 1920 that ended badly for him personally — the school closed within fifteen months and his fortune was gone within eight — and the decision is now part of the founding mythology of the Republic of Korea Air Force. The bet was not safe. It was not diversified. He was not protecting his downside. He gave most of what he had to a 30-cadet flight school in a California rice town because someone told him airplanes would matter in the next war. That kind of conviction shows up rarely. When it does, it tends to outlive the person.
