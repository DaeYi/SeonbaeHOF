# David Hyun (현 데이비드)

**Born:** January 3, 1917 — Seoul, Korea
**Died:** May 2, 2012 — Los Angeles, California
**Field:** Architect, urban planner, community developer

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The first registered Korean American architect — who revitalized Little Tokyo for Japanese Americans before he could ever build the Korea City he imagined for his own people.

## Story

David Hyun was the youngest son of Reverend Soon Hyun, a Methodist minister who helped organize the Korean Provisional Government in Shanghai and served as a cabinet minister in Korea's exiled government during Japanese occupation. The family fled Seoul for Shanghai in 1920, then resettled in Hawaii. David came to Los Angeles in 1947, studied architecture at USC, and in 1950 became the first registered architect in the history of Korean immigration to the United States. He founded David Hyun Associates in 1953 and ran the firm for nearly six decades.

His US career nearly ended before it began. As a union organizer and a vocal critic of US-backed Korean president Syngman Rhee, Hyun was labeled a "dangerous alien" by the federal government, which tried repeatedly to deport him and his family through 1958. Organized community resistance blocked each attempt. He kept practicing.

In the 1970s, the Little Tokyo Redevelopment Project commissioned him to design Japanese Village Plaza — the 89,000-square-foot retail core of LA's Little Tokyo, anchored by the 55-foot red Yagura Fire Tower at its entrance. It opened in 1978, won a 1984 award for excellence in public design (the only LA winner that year), and became the cultural heart of a neighborhood that had been emptied by wartime incarceration. Hyun then turned to his own community, drafting a "Korea City" master plan to do for Koreatown what Japanese Village Plaza had done for Little Tokyo. The plan was never built.

## Decision moments

- Came to Los Angeles in 1947 to study architecture, rather than follow his father's path into ministry or independence politics
- Earned his license in 1950, becoming the first registered Korean American architect — at a moment when there was no Korean American architectural client base to design for
- Kept organizing labor and criticizing Syngman Rhee through the deportation attempts of the 1950s, instead of going quiet to protect his practice
- Took the Little Tokyo commission — a Korean designing for the Japanese American community, a generation after Japanese colonization of Korea
- Drew up Korea City for LA's Koreatown after Japanese Village Plaza succeeded, even though he could not get it financed or built

## Why this matters for the next generation

The most consequential Korean American architect of the 20th century is best known for a Japanese plaza. That is not a failure of the story — it is the story. Hyun's career is a reminder that Korean American work often shows up in places that don't carry a Korean name, and that the dreams Korean Americans draw up for themselves frequently arrive a generation too early. Korea City lives in his archive, not in the streetscape of Koreatown. Someone else's generation has to build it.
