# Mary Paik Lee (백광선)

**Born:** 1900 — Pyongyang, Korean Empire
**Died:** February 14, 1995 — San Francisco, California (age 94)
**Field:** Memoirist, farm laborer, witness to the first Korean American century

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The Korean girl who landed in Hawaii in 1905 and lived long enough — and remembered carefully enough — to give us the only first-person account of an entire Korean American woman's century.

## Story

Mary Paik Lee was five years old in 1905 when Japanese soldiers forced her family out of their Pyongyang home to use it as a barracks. Her father, with no plan and no English, met a sugar plantation recruiter on the run and signed a labor contract that put the family on a ship to Oahu. They worked the cane fields, then borrowed money to cross to California in 1906, joining Dosan Ahn Chang-ho's settlement at Pachappa Camp in Riverside — one-room shacks built decades earlier for Chinese railroad workers, repurposed as the first Koreatown in the United States.

She grew up among Korean farm laborers across California — Riverside, Claremont, Roberts Island, Willows — picking fruit, cooking for crews, attending school in towns where she was the only Asian student. On her first day at Washington Irving School in Riverside, classmates circled her chanting "Ching chong Chinaman." When she complained about scavenging butcher scraps on Saturdays, her father told her to be grateful — the butchers could have thrown the meat away and let everyone starve. That register — flatly observed, never bitter, never softened — became the texture of her memoir.

She married Hung Man Lee on New Year's Day 1919, farmed as tenants, raised three sons, settled in Los Angeles, and lived an ordinary working life that nobody asked her to write about. She was 86 when she sat down anyway and produced a 65-page manuscript. Historian Sucheng Chan recognized what was in her hands — the only full-life autobiography by a Korean American woman covering the era from the 1903–1905 immigration wave forward — and helped her expand it into *Quiet Odyssey: A Pioneer Korean Woman in America*, published by the University of Washington Press in 1990.

## Decision moments

- Survived, as a child, the displacement that her family chose to interpret as an opportunity rather than a defeat — and carried that frame for ninety years
- Stayed in school in towns where she was the only Asian girl, working as a domestic to pay her way through
- Married into Korean farm labor at 18 instead of leaving the community for easier work elsewhere
- In her eighties, volunteered as an interpreter at a Korean senior center in San Francisco for nearly a decade
- At 86, decided her unremarkable life was worth writing down — and was right

## Why this matters for the next generation

History is what gets written down. The 1903–1905 Korean immigration to Hawaii and California produced thousands of lives, almost none of which were recorded by the people who lived them. Mary Paik Lee is the exception. Without her, Pachappa Camp, the citrus-grove labor camps, the small-town schoolyards, the Korean church on a sugar plantation — all of it would exist only in someone else's footnotes. The lesson is small and enormous at once: the lives that look unremarkable while they're happening are often the ones the next century will need. Write it down. Even at 86. Especially at 86.
