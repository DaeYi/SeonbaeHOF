# Ha Soo Whang (황하수)

**Born:** 1892 — Korea
**Died:** 1984
**Field:** Social worker, cultural organizer, dance impresario

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The first Korean social worker in Hawaii — who never danced, but kept Korean dance alive in the islands for two decades by finding the people who could and making sure they had an audience.

## Story

Ha Soo Whang came to the United States as a student in 1911, was educated in missionary schools in Korea and then at Athens College for Young Women in Alabama, and stopped in Hawaii in 1922 on what was supposed to be the trip home. The YWCA International Institute offered her a job. She took it and stayed for the next twenty years. She became the first Korean social worker in the territory, which in practice meant that when a Korean immigrant family in Honolulu needed to talk to a landlord, a doctor, a school principal, a court, or a welfare office, she was the person who showed up to translate — not just the language, but the system on either side of it.

At the Institute she ran two clubs that were structurally identical and pointed in opposite directions. The HyungJay Club taught young Korean American women, born or raised in Hawaii, the traditional culture their parents had brought from Korea. The Mother's Club taught their grandmothers — Korean-born, often illiterate in English — how to navigate American life. She was running a translation service in both directions at once.

Her most lasting contribution was something she could not personally do. She believed Korean dance had to survive in Hawaii, and she could not dance. So she became its agent. She found Susan Chun Lee and Chai Yong Ha, recruited them, arranged for performances, and organized the public showings that kept the form visible in Honolulu until the International Institute was dissolved in 1942 under wartime pressure. The lineage she sustained was picked up after the war by Halla Pai Huhm, whose studio is now the oldest Korean dance studio in the United States. Whang left Hawaii in 1943.

## Decision moments

- Stopped in Hawaii in 1922 on the way back to Korea — and stayed
- Took the social worker role with no template for it, becoming "first" by being the only person who could do the job
- Built the HyungJay and Mother's clubs in parallel — refused to treat assimilation as one-directional
- Promoted Korean dance without being a dancer — recruited Susan Chun Lee and Chai Yong Ha, and put her institutional weight behind their performances
- Kept the work going through Pearl Harbor and into 1942, when the Institute was dissolved
- Left Hawaii in 1943, after twenty years, when the institutional ground for her work had been pulled out

## Why this matters for the next generation

Some of the most important Korean American work is connective, not authored. Ha Soo Whang did not write the books, did not choreograph the dances, did not personally found the YWCA. She was the person who knew everyone, spoke both languages, kept the calendar, and made sure the right people met. That is a real career, and it is often what holds a diaspora community together. The 1.5- and 2nd-gen kid who answers everyone's phone, translates everyone's mail, sits on three nonprofit boards — that person has a predecessor in Ha Soo Whang. Bridge work is its own discipline.
