# Herbert Young Cho Choy (최영조)

**Born:** January 6, 1916 — Makaweli, Kauai, Hawaii
**Died:** March 10, 2004 — Honolulu, Hawaii
**Field:** Federal judge, lawyer

*In memoriam.*

## One-line frame

The son of Korean sugar plantation workers on Kauai who became the first person of Korean ancestry admitted to the US bar — and the first Asian American to sit on a federal court.

## Story

Herbert Choy was born in 1916 on a sugar plantation on Kauai, where his Korean immigrant parents worked the cane fields. Hawaii at that time was the entry point for most of the first wave of Korean labor migration to America — about 7,000 Koreans had arrived between 1903 and 1905 to cut sugar — and the children of those workers were the first generation of Korean Americans expected to break out of plantation life. Choy did. He went to the University of Hawaii, then Harvard Law, graduating in 1941 — by which point he was the first person of Korean ancestry admitted to the bar anywhere in the United States.

The Pearl Harbor attack came months after he passed the bar. He served in the Hawaii Territorial Guard, then the US Army, and finally the Judge Advocate General's Corps. After the war, he went into private practice in Honolulu with Hiram Fong — the man who, fifteen years later, would become the first Asian American US Senator, and who would eventually be the reason Choy got a federal judgeship. From 1957 to 1958 Choy served as the last Attorney General of the Territory of Hawaii, weeks before statehood.

In 1971, on Senator Fong's recommendation, Richard Nixon nominated Choy to the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. At the time, there were no Asian Americans serving anywhere on the federal bench. The Senate confirmed him in two weeks. He served as an active circuit judge until 1984 and on senior status until his death in 2004 — 33 years in total. A former clerk described him in Congressional testimony as possessing "quiet dignity," "scrupulous honesty," and a particular care in immigration cases. He did not seek publicity. He sat, and he wrote, and he stayed.

## Decision moments

- Left the plantation track for a four-year college degree, then for Harvard Law — at a moment when Asian Americans were largely shut out of mainstream legal practice
- Joined the bar in 1941 — there was no precedent, no Korean American legal community, no path
- Partnered with Hiram Fong in private practice for a decade, building the political relationship that would later carry him onto the federal bench
- Took the Territorial Attorney General job in the closing months of Hawaii's territorial status, when statehood was about to render the office obsolete
- Accepted the Nixon nomination as the first Asian American federal judge — a role he could have declined as too symbolic, too exposed, or too political
- Took senior status in 1984 but kept hearing cases until his death twenty years later

## Why this matters for the next generation

There is a kind of trailblazer who makes the most of being first — and there is a kind who quietly does the job for thirty-three years, lets the work speak, and shows up every day. Herbert Choy was the second kind. Korean Americans, more than most communities, are sometimes told that representation is the goal in itself. Choy is the reminder that representation is the floor, not the ceiling — that the actual job is the decades of careful judicial reasoning after the swearing-in, in a robe nobody before you has worn.
